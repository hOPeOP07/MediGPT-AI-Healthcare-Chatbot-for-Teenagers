Epidemiology, it is contended, has had so far a greater influence on our way of eating, acting, living, than any other science or technology (Førde 1998), and overemphasis and overreaction to health risks may be a society problem. One harmful side-effect is that in spite of dramatic improvements in collective health, well-being and satisfaction with personal health are declining, as revealed by surveys in the USA. It has also been suggested that (over)emphasis on healthy lifestyles has led to the increased peril of tyranny of health (Fitzgerald 1994). Health should also mean the ability to cope with ‘unwellness’ in a competent way, and it is argued that the more medicine and society direct individual behaviour, the less autonomous the individual
